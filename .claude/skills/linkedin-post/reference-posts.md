## Post A — Tech Lead mindset shift

The biggest struggle of a new Tech Lead is the mindset shift.
I've seen this many times.
And when it doesn't happen, this becomes a problem for the whole team.

And these are not abstract thoughts.
These are very concrete problems the whole team faces when a Tech Lead keeps acting as an Individual Contributor and doesn't fully understand their new role and the responsibility it requires.

There is no mystery in why this keeps happening.
When a strong technical specialist moves into a Tech Lead role, they have to let go of their old identity and step into a new one.

They need to move away from a comfortable role where they feel confident and clearly know what needs to be done.
And step into a role that is full of uncertainty. A role where they have not yet established themselves.

So there is nothing surprising in the fact that something inside them resists it.

The first step is to acknowledge that this is happening.
To recognise that this internal resistance is there and to create the conditions for overcoming it.

Because if a Tech Lead stays stuck in the old IC jacket that no longer fits them, they will not be able to fully act in the role of a Technical Leader.
And the team will feel it.

**What to learn from it:**
- Hook is a flat claim, no question mark.
- "I've seen this many times." — authority in four words, no credentials paraded.
- Spends the middle explaining the *mechanism* (identity loss, uncertainty, resistance).
- Lands on consequence, not advice: "And the team will feel it."
- Almost no jargon. One metaphor ("IC jacket"), used once.

## Post B — Context over code

The "AI is replacing developers" debate misses the biggest point.

Code is just the output. The real value has always been, and will always be, in the context.

I've been working on building AI-driven delivery pipelines, and the results are eye-opening. Once you provide a high-quality specification and clear business logic, AI handles the heavy lifting: implementation planning, coding, testing, and verification.

But here is the catch: AI cannot guess the intent.

In today's rapid AI development, the most critical skill isn't how fast you type code—it's how well you plan and understand the output you are building. This isn't just an AI-era phenomenon; this has always been the core of engineering, regardless of the industry.

This is exactly where senior developers remain irreplaceable:

Architecting the "Why": Understanding the business requirements and legal constraints that aren't written in any documentation.

Orchestrating the agents: Moving from "coder" to "architect of specifications."

Bridge-building: Navigating inherited systems and stakeholder needs to ensure the output actually solves the right problem.

The real challenge for us isn't the technology—it's the clarity of our specs. If we master that, the execution becomes a solved problem.

The juniors who will struggle are those who treat their role as just "writing code." The ones who treat it as "building context" will be the leaders of the next era.

Agree? Let's discuss in the comments. 👇

**What to learn from it:**
- Hook reframes a tired debate instead of joining it.
- Grounded in real work ("I've been working on building AI-driven delivery pipelines").
- Enumerated section is "Label: explanation", three items, no bullet characters.
- Ends with a stake for the reader, then one short discussion invite plus hashtags.
- Do not copy the ending: "Agree? Let's discuss in the comments. 👇" is what we no longer
  do. The closing line is now a question answerable from experience (see Post C). Also,
  this post has four em dashes; the rule is at most one.

## Post C — Rule that was optional (current skeleton)

Adapted from published post 3 (`linkedIn-posts/3-post.md`), restructured into the six
blocks. Facts are the original's. Two lines are new: the ➡️ sentence and the ❓ question.

⚠️ The AI adoption problem wasn't the rule. It was that the rule was optional.

⛔ At BAT the workflow was clear: write the spec, read the AI output, explain what the code does, test it, then open the PR.
For seniors it clicked. For juniors, "explain what the code does" was the easiest step to skip under deadline pressure.
Nobody caught it until the PR was already open. Sometimes not even then.

➡️ A rule that depends on memory under pressure is a suggestion.

❓ What do you do when the people who need the rule most are the ones least able to follow it on a bad day?

✅ I stopped relying on people to remember it. I put it in the PR template.
No PR opens without a written explanation of what changed and why. No explanation, no review.
The rule didn't change. It stopped depending on discipline and started depending on process.

📚 A checklist doesn't need someone to remember it. It needs someone to build it in.
Where in your workflow are you still trusting discipline instead of enforcing it?

**What to learn from it:**
- One emoji per block, nothing else decorative. Blocks stay short: 1–4 lines.
- The hook is a flat claim that names the real problem, with no question mark.
- ⛔ carries the concrete grounding; ➡️ is a single line, not a paragraph.
- ❓ sits mid-post and is about the reader's situation. The closing question is a
  different line, at the end of 📚, and asks for their experience.
- ✅ switches to "I" at the moment of the decision, and says what changed in the process.
- The image line is separate from the paste-ready text.
